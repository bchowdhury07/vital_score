# Literature Survey: Heart Disease Prediction and Bayesian Calibration

## 1. Baseline ML Studies on Heart Disease Prediction
Machine learning has been extensively applied to heart disease prediction, utilizing various structured clinical datasets such as the UCI Cleveland dataset. Early studies primarily focused on classical algorithms like Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Decision Trees. For instance, Detrano et al. (1989) demonstrated that logistic regression could achieve reasonable accuracy using basic clinical features. More recent benchmarks have shown that non-linear models like SVMs with RBF kernels or ensemble methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting) often yield higher accuracy and ROC-AUC scores by capturing complex interactions among features like age, cholesterol, and maximum heart rate. However, these models frequently output uncalibrated scores rather than true probabilities, limiting their direct clinical utility for risk assessment.

## 2. Bayesian Methods in Clinical Risk Prediction
To address the limitations of point-estimate models, Bayesian approaches have gained traction in medical diagnostics. Bayesian Logistic Regression, in particular, offers a robust framework by placing priors on the model weights and yielding a full posterior distribution of the risk for each patient. This is crucial in clinical settings where uncertainty quantification is just as important as the point prediction itself. Studies utilizing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or Laplace approximations for Bayesian inference have demonstrated that these models gracefully handle small sample sizes and provide natural confidence intervals for their predictions, which helps clinicians understand the reliability of the model's output for an individual patient.

## 3. Calibration and Uncertainty Methodology
A predictive model's clinical value is directly tied to its calibration—how well the predicted probabilities align with the true observed event frequencies. A model is perfectly calibrated if, among the patients given a 20% risk score, exactly 20% experience the event. Many modern non-linear models (e.g., SVMs, deep neural networks) are notorious for poor calibration, often pushing predictions too close to 0 or 1. To correct this, researchers commonly apply post-hoc calibration techniques like Platt scaling (fitting a logistic regression on the outputs) or Isotonic Regression on a hold-out validation set.

## 4. Project Differentiation
This project differs fundamentally from the closest prior work, such as the recent paper "Model uncertainty quantification: A post hoc calibration approach for heart disease prediction" (medRxiv 2025). While that study relies on **post-hoc calibration** to correct the overconfident point estimates of traditional machine learning models after they have been trained, our approach utilizes a **native Bayesian posterior**. By implementing Bayesian Logistic Regression via MCMC, our model naturally incorporates uncertainty directly into the parameter estimation phase. This means the resulting probabilities are intrinsically calibrated and provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for uncertainty without requiring a separate, hold-out post-hoc calibration step.
